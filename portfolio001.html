The Six Majors

Published 11th November 2026 Interactive ~4 min read

The World Marathon Majors are six races and one idea. Tokyo, Boston, London, Berlin, Chicago and New York each crown a men's winner and a women's winner, then do it again the next year. A table of those names is easy to collect and hard to feel. I wanted the same list as a place you can turn.

The dashboard below puts every winning run from 2000 to 2026 on a globe. The six cities are pins. Click one and the wins travel in as arcs, thicker where a country keeps coming back. Scrub the year and the map, the chart and the repeat-winner list all move together. Click a name and the view follows that athlete from race to race.

In this cut of the data: 300 winning runs, 158 different athletes, and Kenya out in front with 162 of those wins. Eliud Kipchoge alone accounts for 11.

Tokyo is the youngest pin. Its first edition in the file is 2007, so it carries fewer races than the other five, and the pin is drawn a little smaller because of it. The rest of the cities run from 2000. A few years are missing on purpose: Boston, Berlin and Chicago in 2020, and New York in 2012.

How I did it

This one is not a Tableau workbook. It is a single HTML file: the globe, the side panel and the winner rows all live in six-majors-dashboard.html. Nothing has to be built or served. The same file is what you are looking at above, and what opens when you take it full screen.

The board itself is a fixed 1400 by 800 layout. A small fit step scales that board to whatever window it is given, which is why the iframe and the full-screen page can share one design. The globe is a canvas. d3 supplies an orthographic projection, the sort that looks like a sphere rather than a flat map, and a TopoJSON world provides the land and the borders. The six cities are dots on top. Drag to spin, scroll or pinch to zoom, and if reduced motion is on, the globe stays still instead of turning by itself.

Opening a race does three things at once. The globe turns to face that city. Each winning country sends an arc to the pin, with the stroke weight following the number of wins, and men and women are bowed apart so the two series do not sit on the same line. A country that is already on top of the race, Japan at Tokyo for example, is drawn as rings on the pin. An arc that short would collapse into a smudge.

The panel is the table I did not want drawn on the globe. Fastest man, fastest woman, most wins, and how many nations have won it. Under that, a year slider plays the race forward. The time chart puts faster times higher up the page, marks world records and course records, and leaves a cross where an edition did not happen. New York's 2020 virtual race stays in the data, and stays out of that chart. The bars beside it are wins by country up to the year you are on, and the list next to them is anyone with two or more.

Click a name and the race panel gives way to the athlete. A dashed line runs from their country through the majors they won, in the order they won them. Hover a leg and that stop's years and times come up. Nationality and birthplace are both stored on each row, and the toggle redraws the arcs from one or the other. Where a birthplace was never recorded, it falls back to nationality. A couple of spellings are joined up too, so Robert Cheruiyot and Atsede Bayisa are not split into two people.

There is no framework and no build step. d3 draws the map and the chart. The rest is the page.

The Last Thread

A globe is a poor place for a table, and a table is a poor place for a route. The useful bit was letting one click move you from the city to the person, with the years still in reach. Six pins is the whole geography. The story is who keeps turning up at them.
